## Learning Outcomes (Expanded)

### Why Hands-On Learning Works
- **Spatial skills can be trained, and children benefit most.** A meta-analysis of 217 studies (Uttal et al., 2013, *Psychological Bulletin*) found spatial training produced an average effect of g = 0.47 versus control groups. Gains lasted over time and transferred to untrained spatial tasks. A later review notes the effect rose to about 0.61 for children under 13.
- **Puzzle play predicts spatial ability.** University of Chicago research (Levine et al., 2012, *Developmental Science*) followed 53 children and found puzzle play was a significant predictor of later spatial skill (mental rotation and shape transformation), even after controlling for parents' income, education and language input. The authors note the findings are correlational.
- **Spatial ability predicts STEM success.** Uttal et al. cite Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2009) and Shea, Lubinski & Benbow (2001): strong spatial skills predict achievement and attainment in science, technology, engineering and maths.
- **Trial and error builds persistence.** The manufacturer positions Kanoodle Genius as developing "perseverance" and "flexible thinking" through "learning through trial and error".

### STEM Skills

**Spatial Reasoning:**
- Mental rotation: picturing how a piece looks when turned or flipped before placing it
- Visualising how shapes fit into a remaining gap (shape composition and decomposition)
- 3D visualisation: stacking pieces into pyramid layers, thinking about pieces that span levels

**Logic & Problem-Solving:**
- Constraint-based reasoning: the starting pieces fix the rules; work out what can fill what's left
- Elimination and hypothesis testing: try a placement, see if the rest can still fit, backtrack
- Planning ahead: noticing awkward gaps early (dead ends) before they trap you

**Maths Foundations:**
- Geometry: shapes, symmetry, rotation, reflection
- Counting and area thinking: matching the number of empty holes to the pieces left
- Pattern recognition across puzzles

**Engineering Thinking:**
- Structural stacking in the 3D pyramid puzzles
- Iterating on a design until it works

- Careers that rely on spatial and logical reasoning: engineering, architecture, surgery, pilots, game design, packaging and logistics, coding and algorithm design
- Fitting shapes into a space is the same kind of problem as packing, scheduling and "constraint satisfaction" problems in computer science (polyomino-style puzzles are a classic example)
