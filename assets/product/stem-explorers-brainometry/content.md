### How It Works (gameplay)
- Pick a challenge card. Each card face shows a 2D pattern / picture of a structure.
- Use the 3D shape pieces to recreate it. Kids have to visualise how flat pictures translate into 3D pieces, choose the right shapes, colours and patterns, and stack/arrange them.
- Test different solutions until it matches ("test different solutions until everything clicks into place").
- Challenges get progressively harder: early puzzles ease players into shape placement; later ones demand stronger spatial reasoning.
- After finishing the cards, kids design and build their own structures and puzzles (reviewers mention kids building their own castles and forts).
- Played solo (Amazon: Number of Players 1), but works for siblings taking turns, small groups in class, or a parent and child together.

## Learning Outcomes (Expanded)

### Why Hands-On Learning Works
- **Spatial skills predict later maths.** Research summarised by the University of Washington I-LABS reports that children's spatial skills at age 3 predicted their maths skills at age 5, and were a stronger predictor than early counting skills. (https://modules.ilabs.uw.edu/module/spatial-skills-and-reasoning/spatial-skills-and-early-math-ability/)
- **Copying block structures links to early maths.** A study by Temple University and University of Delaware researchers (Verdine et al., *Child Development*, 2014; covered by ScienceDaily 2013) found that 3-year-olds who were better at copying block structures from a model were also better at early maths. This is very close to what Brainometry asks children to do (recreate a pictured structure with blocks). (https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/09/130927105018.htm)
- **Puzzle play builds spatial transformation skills.** Levine et al. (2012), "Early Puzzle Play: A Predictor of Preschoolers' Spatial Transformation Skill" (University of Chicago, 53 child–parent pairs): children who played with puzzles between 26 and 46 months had better ability to mentally rotate and translate shapes at 54 months, even after controlling for parent income, education and language input. Observational, so association not proven causation. (https://news.uchicago.edu/story/puzzle-play-helps-boost-learning-important-math-related-skills)
- **Spatial skills can be trained, and gains last.** Uttal et al. (2012), "The Malleability of Spatial Skills: A Meta-Analysis of Training Studies", *Psychological Bulletin*: 217 studies; average training effect size g = 0.47; gains were durable over time and transferred to untrained spatial tasks. (https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2012/08/uttal-spatial-thinking)
- **Block building involves mental rotation and shape features.** A Frontiers in Psychology (2019) review explains that during block building children learn how objects vary in size, pattern, symmetry and shape, and practise mental rotation as they align and rotate blocks. (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01626/pdf)
- **Key insight:** Brainometry combines three research-backed activities in one: copying a model structure, puzzle-style shape fitting, and translating between 2D pictures and 3D objects.

### Future-Ready Skills & Career Connections
- **Spatial ability and STEM careers:** Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2009), "Spatial Ability for STEM Domains", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 101(4), 817–835. Using Project Talent data (~400,000 US high school students tracked for 11+ years), spatial ability strongly predicted who went into and succeeded in STEM fields, beyond maths and verbal ability. A secondary summary reports ~90% of STEM PhD graduates in the sample came from the top quartile of spatial ability (not verified against the original paper). (https://www.Gwern.net/doc/iq/high/smpy/2009-wai.pdf)
- **Careers that rely on spatial thinking:** engineering, architecture, surgery and medicine, pilots, graphic and product design, robotics, geology, chemistry (molecular structures), game design, 3D modelling.
- **Early start matters:** Uttal et al. show spatial skills are trainable at any age, and Levine/Verdine work shows the preschool and early primary years are when these skills start to separate children.
