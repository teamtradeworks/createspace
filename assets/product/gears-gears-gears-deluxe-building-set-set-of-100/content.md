## Learning Outcomes (Expanded)

### Why Hands-On Learning Works

- **Spatial skills predict early maths.** Verdine et al. (2013/2014, *Child Development*) tested 102 three-year-olds copying block models. Spatial assembly ability independently predicted concurrent maths performance after controlling for vocabulary (partial r = .30; match scores explained about 16% of variance in early maths scores), with the strongest link to counting (partial r = .45). The authors describe spatial thinking as potentially crucial for later STEM learning and note spatial skills can be trained. No gender differences were found. Children from lower-SES homes already scored lower at age 3, and parents' use of spatial words correlated with both spatial (r = .33) and maths (r = .42) scores, a case for talking through builds together.
- **Spatial skills are malleable.** The same paper cites Uttal et al. (2013) meta-analysis showing spatial skills improve with practice, and Casey et al. (2008) showing goal-oriented block building improved Block Design scores.
- **Manipulable parts spark engineering thinking.** Gold (2014, Purdue University thesis) observed 66 preschoolers aged 3 to 5 and found children engaged in "engineering thinking play" about three times as often with large manipulable loose parts as on a traditional playground, with no difference between girls and boys. Loose-parts play also involved high levels of fine and gross motor activity and positive social behaviour.
- **Design play builds spatial thinking for every play style.** Sonneveld, Klapwijk & Stappers (2024, *Frontiers in Education*, TU Delft) studied 49 preschoolers aged 4 to 6 and found spatial thinking was practised in every play orientation, both "construction" and "pretend" players. Story-based design tasks drew in children who usually avoid construction. Quote: "design activities stimulate children to practice spatial thinking in a creative context."
- **Engineering play** (Mediterranean Journal of Education, 2024): during engineering play children both apply and further develop pre-engineering thinking.

### STEM Skills

**Early engineering and mechanics:**
- How gears mesh and transfer motion from one to the next (cause and effect)
- Neighbouring gears turn in opposite directions (EX-GEAR-IMENT "How Does It Spin?")
- Motion travels along a chain of gears ("End to End")
- Turning motion can change direction through 90° (gear on a vertical axle meshing with a gear on the base, "Going Up")
- Using a crank (handle) as an input to drive a whole system
- Building stable structures: bases, pillars, axles; vertical and horizontal building

**Early maths:**
- Counting, sorting, matching and patterning by colour and size
- Spatial relationships: up, over, beside, next to, in a row
- Sequencing (building step by step; sequential thought)
- Shapes and arrangements of bases (rows, pyramids, zigzags)

**Scientific thinking:**
- Predicting and testing ("Are the gears spinning in the same direction?" "Does the last gear start spinning right away?")
- Observation of movement and connections
- Trial and error when gears don't mesh

**Physical development:**
- Fine motor strength and control (pushing gears onto pegs, joining bases)
- Eye-hand coordination
- Visual processing and colour identification

| # | Name | Type | Description (verbatim from guide) | Skills/Concepts Taught |
|---|------|------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | Build a Base | Activity (Building Basics) | "Attach bases together by sliding the side pegs into the slots. There are many different ways to arrange the bases: rows, pyramids, zigzags, and more!" | Fine motor, spatial arrangement, shapes, planning |
| 2 | Add Gears | Activity (Building Basics) | "Line up the center hole of a gear with the top peg of a base, and pop it on! | 3 | Get Things Moving | Activity (Building Basics) | "Turn the crank to start everything moving. The crank works best if placed flat on a gear that is attached to a base, and not a vertical structure." | Input/output, cause and effect, systems |
| 4 | Going Up | Activity (Building Basics) | "Begin vertical structures by placing a pillar into the square hole on a base. Then, add a six-way axle and attach gears to the axle, making sure the "teeth" mesh at a right angle with the gear on the base. The gear on the axle should spin when you turn the gear on the base." | 3D building, changing direction of motion (right-angle gears), spatial reasoning |
| 5 | Up and Over | Activity (Building Basics) | "Continue to build up by adding a pillar to the top of the six-way axle and attaching another six-way axle. Add a pillar to the side of a six-way axle to build horizontally." | Vertical and horizontal structures, stability, spatial relationships |
| 6 | How Does It Spin? | Challenge (EX-GEAR-IMENT) | "Place two gears next to each other on a base. Spin the gears and watch them move. Are the gears spinning in the same direction?" | Observation, prediction, direction of rotation (meshed gears turn opposite ways) |
| 7 | End to End | Challenge (EX-GEAR-IMENT) | "Connect the bases so that they are in a straight line. Make a long row of connecting gears going from one end of the bases to the other. Spin the first gear at one end very slowly. Does the last gear start spinning right away?" | Gear trains, transfer of motion, cause and effect, patience/observation |

Guide framing for the EX-GEAR-IMENTS (verbatim): "The following EX-GEAR-IMENTS encourage children to notice interesting movements and connections, and to explore the fascinating action that can be created with gears."

**Difficulty Progression:** Ordered by difficulty. Flat building first (bases, then gears, then crank), then vertical/3D building (pillars and six-way axles), then two investigation questions. After that, play is entirely open-ended.

**Note:** The larger Super Building Set (LER9164, 150 pieces) uses an identical guide (same Building Basics and same two EX-GEAR-IMENTS), so moving up in the range adds pieces, not new guide content.

### Life Skills & Character Development

**Critical Thinking & Problem-Solving:**
- Working out why a gear won't turn (teeth not meshing, gear too far away) and fixing it
- Predicting what will happen before turning the crank, then checking
- Becker's: children "intuitively use problem-solving and engineering skills"

**Resilience & Growth Mindset:**
- Builds often need adjusting before everything spins; small, quick fixes reward persistence
- Towers can topple; rebuilding is part of play

**Emotional Regulation:**
- Satisfying, repetitive spinning motion; calm, focused play
- Some frustration possible with stiff base connections for very young builders (an adult can help at first)

**Confidence & Self-Esteem:**
- Immediate visible result: turn the crank and the whole creation moves
- Simple enough for a 3-year-old to succeed in the first few minutes

**Creativity & Innovation:**
- Open-ended; the guide explicitly invites children to "jump right in and construct your own design"
- Many base layouts and vertical/horizontal combinations

**Social Skills:**
- Suited to 1 to 4 children building together (STEMfinity); good for siblings and preschool groups

### Future-Ready Skills & Career Connections

- Gears are the foundation of mechanical engineering: bicycles, clocks, cars, wind turbines, food mixers, robots.
- In South Africa's CAPS curriculum, mechanical systems appear in Natural Sciences & Technology from Grade 4 to 5 ("Systems for moving things": wheels, axles, gears, levers, pulleys) and formally in Technology from Grade 7 (Mechanical Systems and Control: levers, pulleys, gears, linkages) and Grade 8 ("Wedges, wheels and gears"). Early hands-on play gives children an intuitive head start on these topics.
- Engineering skills are in short supply in South Africa: Xpatweb's 2025 Critical Skills Survey (381 employers) found 38% struggled to find engineers in 2025, up from 23% in 2024 and 14% in 2018; shortages ranged from maintenance and industrial engineers to mechanical engineering technicians. The September 2026 preliminary results still list engineering as the most in-demand skill (29% citing a shortage), with demand for artisans the highest since 2017.
- Spatial skills built early are linked with later maths and STEM achievement (Verdine et al.).
