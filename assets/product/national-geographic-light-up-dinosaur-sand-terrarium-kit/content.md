## Learning Outcomes (Expanded)

### Why Hands-On Learning Works

- **Intense interests (dinosaurs):** Research led by Joyce M. Alexander (Indiana University), with University of Wisconsin researchers, found that roughly 30–50% of preschool children develop an "intense interest" at some point, and dinosaurs are among the most common. Conceptual interests (wanting to know how dinosaurs lived, what they ate) are associated with longer attention spans, greater persistence, and more complex information processing. Researchers quoted as finding such interests "increase persistence, improve attention, and enhance skills complex thinking when processing information." Caveat: these are correlations, and popular headlines ("dinosaur kids are smarter") overstate them. A UK report notes that only around 20% of children keep their intense interest after starting school, so feeding it while it lasts matters.
- **Pretend / small-world play and storytelling:** A randomised double-blind study (90 children aged 5–6) found joint adult-child pretend play ("PlayWorld") supports narrative development; earlier work found significant improvements in story length, coherence and comprehension. Nicolopoulou's research suggests pretend play lays groundwork for later storytelling skills. Caveat: Lillard et al. (2013, *Psychological Bulletin*) concluded the evidence does not support strong causal claims about pretend play's unique importance, though narrative links are among the better-supported.
- **Sand play:** Early years practitioners and landscape-architecture literature (ASLA) link sand play to sensory, fine-motor, cognitive, social and verbal development, and to calming and emotional regulation. A 2021 systematic review protocol (IJERPH) notes the peer-reviewed evidence is still thin, so present these as widely observed benefits rather than proven outcomes.
- **Rocks and minerals:** The American Geosciences Institute notes that very young children naturally enjoy picking up rocks, sorting them, choosing favourites and wetting them to make them shine, and frames this as an entry point to learning about the planet. The South Australian Museum's early years geology kit lists classifying, observing and comparing as the core science process skills developed by handling minerals. A Spanish early-years study used rock and mineral lessons with 3-year-olds to build motivation for geology.

### STEM Skills

**Palaeontology & Earth Science:**
- Dinosaurs and the prehistoric world (facts in the Learning Guide)
- Habitats: what a living environment needs and how animals fit into it
- The Earth changes over time (prehistoric scenes vs today)

**Geology & Minerals (real specimens):**
- Rose quartz: a pink variety of quartz (silicon dioxide), coloured by traces of titanium; hard, about 7 on the Mohs scale
- Green aventurine: also a quartz-family stone; its sparkle ("aventurescence") comes from tiny mica inclusions, often fuchsite, which also gives the green colour; about 6.5–7 on the Mohs scale
- Blue calcite: not a quartz; it is calcium carbonate, much softer (3 on the Mohs scale), and can glow under UV light
- River stone: rounded and smoothed by water over time (erosion)
- Comparing colour, shine, texture and hardness; sorting and classifying

**Design & Engineering Thinking:**
- Planning a layout before building (choosing a backdrop, layering pebbles then sand)
- Layering materials and sculpting landforms (hills, valleys, riverbeds)
- Spatial reasoning: arranging objects in a 3D space viewed from all sides
- Light and colour: how lighting changes how a scene looks
