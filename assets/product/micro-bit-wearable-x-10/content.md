### Product Overview

The BBC micro:bit Wearable is an official accessory designed to make the BBC micro:bit body-worn and portable. It consists of two parts: a flexible TPE rubber holder that grips the micro:bit and battery pack together, and an adjustable hook-and-loop (Velcro-style) fabric strap that attaches the whole unit to the wrist, ankle, or any object.

The pack of 10 is specifically designed for classroom, club, and group use — giving every learner in a group their own wearable unit simultaneously. Assembly instructions are printed on the side of the box.

This product is compatible with both BBC micro:bit V1 and V2.2 and works with all micro:bit coding platforms.

## Learning Outcomes (Expanded)

### Why Wearable STEM Learning Works

Wearable technology projects are particularly powerful in STEM education because they make abstract computing concepts tangible and personally relevant. When a child straps a micro:bit to their wrist and sees the LED matrix respond to their movement, coding becomes immediately real. Research published in the *Journal of Science Education and Technology* found that wearable textiles provide hands-on learning that is both exciting and personally relevant, with positive effects on STEM attitudes — particularly notable for female learners who historically engage more strongly with aesthetics and design contexts.

A 2023 systematic review in *Frontiers in Education* found that student learning improved across a wide range of subjects and age groups through wearable devices, from K-12 to higher education. The research highlights improved cognitive engagement, curiosity, and real-world problem-solving as consistent outcomes.

The BBC micro:bit platform specifically has strong evidence of impact:
- **90%** of 11–12 year olds who used a micro:bit said it showed them that anyone can code
- **88%** said it helped them see coding wasn't as difficult as they thought
- **80%** of students felt anyone could learn programming after using the micro:bit
- **90%** of teachers would use the micro:bit again in their classroom
- **84%** or more teachers reported increased confidence in teaching computing after using micro:bit resources

### STEM Skills Developed

**Physical Computing & Hardware:**
- Understanding how sensors capture real-world data
- Connecting software outputs to physical actions
- Prototyping and iteration

**Programming Concepts:**
- Event-driven programming (respond to gestures, shakes, steps)
- Variables and data storage
- Loops and conditionals
- Wireless communication between devices (radio/Bluetooth)
- Data logging and analysis

**Electronics & Engineering:**
- How accelerometers, magnetometers, and temperature sensors work
- Circuit completion through conductive materials (e-textiles)
- Microcontroller programming

**Mathematics & Data:**
- Measurement and data collection
- Understanding axes (X, Y, Z motion)
- Threshold values and conditional logic

**AI & Emerging Technologies:**
- Machine learning training with body movement data (via CreateAI)
- Gesture recognition model building
- Introduction to IoT concepts

### Life Skills & Character Development

**Critical Thinking & Problem-Solving:**
- Debugging code when the step counter doesn't work as expected
- Iterating on designs when the strap doesn't sit correctly during movement
- Testing hypotheses about which axis registers which movement

**Resilience & Growth Mindset:**
- STEM projects naturally involve failure and retry cycles — children who work through this develop resilience that transfers beyond the classroom
- Research shows children who engage with hands-on STEM "learn resilience, understanding that failure isn't the end but an opportunity to analyse, learn, and try again"
- The wearable format adds stakes and context — your code has to work in the real world, on your body, while moving

**Confidence & Self-Esteem:**
- Wearing a coded device they programmed themselves is a high-visibility achievement
- 88% of micro:bit users report increased confidence that coding is something they can do
- Completing increasingly complex challenges builds mastery and genuine self-efficacy

**Creativity & Innovation:**
- Projects like the Super Cape and Electro Fingers glove blend engineering with creative expression
- Open-ended wearable challenges allow multiple valid solutions
- Children design functional wearables for real-world use cases

**Collaboration:**
- Classroom pack of 10 enables paired and group work
- Projects like the Multiplayer Escape Room require coordinating multiple devices
- Students take on different roles (coder, tester, designer)

### Future-Ready Skills & Career Connections

The micro:bit wearable directly builds skills relevant to some of the most in-demand careers of the next decade:

- **IoT Engineer** — The IoT job market spans healthcare, manufacturing, utilities, transportation, agriculture, and consumer products; micro:bit wearables teach the foundational concepts of connected devices and sensor data
- **Wearable Technology Developer** — LinkedIn reports 1,000+ open wearable technology jobs; companies like Apple, Fitbit, Garmin, and medical device makers seek developers who understand how to code wearable experiences
- **Embedded Systems Developer** — The embedded systems field is expanding rapidly due to IoT, automotive electronics, medical devices, and smart home technologies
- **Biomedical Engineer** — Projects like the rehabilitation game and biofeedback system introduce concepts directly applicable to medical device design
- **UX/Creative Technologist** — E-textile and wearable projects blend aesthetic design with functional engineering, a skill set in high demand

An IEEE-published study found that IoT and wearable hackathons significantly increased students' motivation to pursue computer science careers, with students developing stronger understanding of design processes, programming, and hardware-software integration.

### What Parents/Educators Will Observe

Children who regularly engage with wearable micro:bit projects typically demonstrate:
- **Greater ownership of learning** — wearing something they coded creates a personal investment in making it work
- **Increased perseverance** — motion-sensing projects don't always work first try; children learn to debug
- **Cross-subject connections** — step counting connects to maths; temperature monitoring connects to science; the Walking for Water project connects to global studies
- **Confidence talking about technology** — children can explain how accelerometers work, how the board detects movement, and what their code does
- **Interest in how everyday wearables work** — smartwatches, fitness trackers, and health monitors become less mysterious and more approachable
