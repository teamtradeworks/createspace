## Learning Outcomes (Expanded)

### Why Hands-On Learning Works
- **Spatial skills can be trained, and the gains last and transfer.** A meta-analysis of 217 studies (Uttal, Meadow, Tipton, Hand, Alden, Warren & Newcombe, *Psychological Bulletin*, 2013, DOI 10.1037/a0028446) found spatial skills are malleable. Even small amounts of training improved spatial reasoning with long-lasting effects, for children and adults, male and female, and "spatial training transfers to other fields." Training may be especially effective for young children and for those starting with weaker spatial skills. Nora Newcombe: "People of all ages can improve at all types of spatial skills through training, period." (Temple University news release)
- **Spatial ability predicts STEM success.** Wai, Lubinski & Benbow (2009), "Spatial Ability for STEM Domains: Aligning Over 50 Years of Cumulative Psychological Knowledge Solidifies Its Importance" (*Journal of Educational Psychology*, 101(4), 817-835). This study followed about 400,000 US high-school students for 11+ years and found spatial ability plays a critical role in developing STEM expertise, beyond maths and verbal ability. Talent searches that ignore spatial ability miss many students with STEM potential.
- **Puzzle play is linked to stronger spatial skills.** Levine, Ratliff, Huttenlocher & Cannon (2012), "Early Puzzle Play: A Predictor of Preschoolers' Spatial Transformation Skill" (*Developmental Psychology*, 48, 530-542). The study followed 53 parent-child pairs. Children who played with puzzles did better on tasks that required rotating and translating shapes. This held after controlling for parental income, education and language input. The study was observational, so it shows correlation, not proof of cause. University of Chicago release: "The ability to mentally transform shapes is an important predictor of STEM" and "engaging both boys and girls in puzzle play can support the development" of these skills. The study is about toddlers, so it supports the general case for puzzles rather than Kanoodle specifically.
- **Polyomino puzzles specifically.** Kanoodle pieces are polyomino-style shapes, like pentominoes. A Thai quasi-experimental study (60 lower-secondary students, Pentomino Puzzle Program) found significantly higher spatial-ability scores after the programme (p < .01). Evidence specific to pentominoes is small-scale, so lean on the broader spatial-training research above.

### STEM Skills

**Spatial Reasoning & Geometry:**
- Mental rotation: picturing how a piece looks when turned or flipped before placing it
- Visualising how 2-D shapes fill an area (tiling/tessellation) and how pieces stack into a 3-D pyramid
- Seeing the same piece work flat (2-D) and stacked horizontally or vertically (3-D)
- Area and volume intuition: every board has 55 spaces, and all 12 pieces must fill them exactly

**Logic & Mathematical Thinking:**
- Process of elimination: ruling out pieces and orientations that can't fit
- Constraint reasoning: working around fixed pieces that "Do not move"
- Systematic trial and error and backtracking
- Pattern recognition: spotting which gap shapes match which pieces

**Problem-Solving Strategy:**
- Breaking a problem into parts (fill the awkward corners first)
- Planning ahead and predicting the effect of each move
- Strategic and flexible thinking (manufacturer: "promotes flexible thinking")

### Future-Ready Skills & Career Connections
- Spatial ability is a strong, independent predictor of STEM achievement and entry into STEM careers (Wai, Lubinski & Benbow 2009). It's often overlooked in school testing, which focuses on maths and verbal ability.
- Careers that rely on mental rotation and spatial planning include engineering, architecture, surgery and medicine, product and industrial design, game design and 3-D modelling, geology, chemistry (molecular structures), pilots, and logistics/packing optimisation.
- The polyomino tiling at the heart of Kanoodle is a classic problem in mathematics and computer science (exact-cover problems, solved by algorithms such as Knuth's "Dancing Links"). It's a hook for older children who move on to coding.
