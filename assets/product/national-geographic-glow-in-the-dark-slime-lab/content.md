## Learning Outcomes (Expanded)

### Why Hands-On Learning Works
- A 2025 systematic review (Waters, Yang & Harradine, via STEM Innovation for Inclusion in Early Education, UNC) found play-based learning to be a key practice for building STEM identity from birth to kindergarten: it "provides a hands-on, engaging way for young children to explore STEM concepts, fostering their interest, confidence, and a sense of enjoyment."
- Cohen et al. (2021, *Science Education*), using survey data from 15,725 college students, found early STEM experiences can be formative for STEM identity years later, "despite intervening years of changing STEM interest."
- A randomised trial of 24 weeks of hands-on, museum-delivered STEM activities (Frontiers in Education, 2025) concluded informal hands-on experiences can positively influence elementary children's STEM-related attitudes.
- Sensory/tactile play: widely claimed to support fine motor skills and focus, but no robust peer-reviewed data was found specifically for slime. Avoid quoting statistics here.

### STEM Skills

**Polymer chemistry:**
- Slime is a polymer: long chain molecules (typically PVA or guar gum) that become cross-linked by a borate (from borax) into a stretchy network
- Adding water to the powder lets the chains hydrate and link, turning a powder into a gooey solid-liquid
- Non-Newtonian behaviour: slime flows slowly when left alone but resists or snaps when pulled quickly
- Concept of a chemical reaction / change of state that kids can see and feel

**Light and luminescence (physics):**
- Phosphorescence: glow pigments (phosphors, commonly strontium aluminate or zinc sulfide) absorb light energy and release it slowly as visible light in the dark
- Brighter/longer light exposure gives a brighter, longer glow (guidance from Blue Marble's related Mega Slime Kit manual: charge under strong light for several minutes; for a weak glow, expose for 5 to 10 minutes)
- Difference between phosphorescence (stored light, like this slime) and chemiluminescence (glow sticks)
- Modern phosphors are non-radioactive and safe

**Biology: slime in nature (learning guide topics):**
- Slime molds: single-celled organisms that behave in surprising ways. *Physarum polycephalum* found the shortest path through a maze between two food sources (Nakagaki, Yamada & Tóth, *Nature*, 2000) and grew a network resembling the Tokyo rail system (Tero et al., *Science*, 2010)
- Hagfish: about 100 glands along the body release a gel that expands in seawater into a large mass of slime that clogs a predator's mouth and gills in under half a second (National Geographic). The slime is about 99.996% seawater, held together by mucin and protein fibres. Hagfish tie themselves in a knot to scrape slime off.
- Snails: use mucus to glide and grip as they move
- Slime as an animal adaptation (defence, movement)

**Scientific method:**
- Following written instructions in sequence
- Observing changes (texture, stretch, glow brightness and duration)
- Testing variables: light source, charging time, how long the glow lasts
